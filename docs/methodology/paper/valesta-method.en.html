Sales comparison with homogenisation coefficients: the valuation method of Valesta

Status
Draft for technical review
Date
24 November 2026
Publisher
Domestone S.r.l.

Abstract

Valesta is an open-source application for the valuation of residential and other property. Its main method is a sales comparison in which the differences between the subject and each comparable are expressed as multiplicative homogenisation coefficients on an ordinal five-level scale. This paper sets out the method formally: the cleansing of comparable prices (negotiation, time and annexes), the homogenisation of unit prices, the weighting of comparables by source reliability and similarity, the choice of estimator, the confidence interval of the estimate with an effective sample size, and the conversion of the unit value into a market value. It then describes how Valesta reports the uncertainty that comes from the valuer’s judgement, with a leave-one-out analysis and a Monte Carlo simulation over the judgement inputs, and how other approaches are reconciled. A synthetic worked example, computed by the code described, shows every step. The paper relates the method to the international and national standards and to hedonic price theory, and states its limits: the default coefficients are expert values that are not calibrated on market data, the ratings are ordinal and partly judgemental, and the confidence interval measures the precision of the estimate, not the range of prices at which the property could sell.

Keywords: property valuation; sales comparison approach; market approach; homogenisation coefficients; adjustment grid; hedonic prices; effective sample size; valuation uncertainty; Monte Carlo simulation.

1. Introduction

The market approach estimates value by comparison with prices of similar properties. The International Valuation Standards list it among the valuation approaches (IVS 103, Valuation Approaches) [IVSC-2025], the RICS Red Book states that it aligns with those standards [RICS-RB-2025], and the European Valuation Standards are published by TEGoVA [TEGOVA-EVS-2025]. How far these standards constrain the technique of adjustment must be checked on their texts (content to be verified); national practice, in any case, differs: the Spanish order on valuations for financial purposes requires homogenisation with justified criteria, coefficients and weights, on information from at least six transactions or offers [ES-ECO805]; the German valuation regulation adjusts comparison prices with indices and conversion coefficients derived from purchase prices [DE-IMMOWERTV]; in Italy the name «market comparison approach» is commonly given to an adjustment by marginal prices of the characteristics [IT-UNI11612] [IT-CVI] (to be checked on the texts, which are paywalled).

Valesta implements one comparison technique, which we call sales comparison with homogenisation coefficients. Each difference between the subject and a comparable is rated on a five-level scale and turned into a coefficient; the product of the coefficients homogenises the comparable’s unit price; a weighted estimator of the homogenised prices gives the unit value. The technique is close to the homogenisation of the Spanish order and has the structure of the German comparison method, but its coefficients are expert defaults, not estimates from market data.

The contribution of this paper is a complete and checkable statement of the method: every formula is given with its parameters, every threshold is named as a convention when it is one, and every number of the worked example is produced by the published code. The software, its tests and a longer technical documentation are available in the same repository (see Reproducibility).

Section 2 summarises the background. Section 3 defines the model. Section 4 describes the analysis of the uncertainty due to judgement, and section 5 the other approaches and their reconciliation. Section 6 is the worked example. Section 7 discusses the properties, the limits and the calibration of the method, and section 8 concludes.

A note on sources. Every reference cited carries, in the Valesta bibliography, the date and the scope of its verification. Where a statement rests on a source whose content could not be checked on the text (only its bibliographic data were), it is marked «content to be verified».

2. Background

2.1 Adjustment methods

[PAGOURTZI-2003] reviews the main valuation methods. Within the comparison approach, three ways of handling the differences between properties are relevant here.

  • Adjustment grids. In United States practice the sales comparison approach adjusts each comparable’s price for each element of comparison and reconciles the adjusted prices; comparables that need smaller adjustments are generally given more weight [AI-ARE-16] (content to be verified).
  • Coefficients. The Spanish order homogenises comparables by «criterios, coeficientes y/o ponderaciones», which must be justified (art. 22.1.d) [ES-ECO805]; the German regulation uses conversion coefficients (§ 19) and indices (§ 18) derived by official bodies from purchase prices [DE-IMMOWERTV].
  • Hedonic prices. Hedonic theory treats a property as a bundle of characteristics and estimates the implicit price of each from observed prices [ROSEN-1974] (content to be verified). A log-linear hedonic model, ln P = α + Σj βj xj + ε, implies that two properties differing by Δxj in characteristic j have prices in the ratio exp(βj Δxj).

Valesta’s coefficients can be read as a first-order version of the hedonic ratio (section 3.4), with the difference that their slopes are fixed in advance rather than estimated.

2.2 Valuation uncertainty

Valuation uncertainty is the doubt about the figure reported at the valuation date, which the literature distinguishes from the variation between valuers and over time [FRENCH-GABRIELLI-2004] (content to be verified). Statistical dispersion of the comparables is only one of its sources; the inputs set by the valuer are another. Valesta reports the first with a confidence interval (section 3.7) and the second with a sensitivity analysis (section 4).

3. The model

3.1 Data and notation

The valuation has a subject, with reference area S0, and n comparables i = 1, …, n that the valuer has retained. Each comparable has a price Pi, an area Si, a date, a source and the ratings nij of J factors j = 1, …, J. Valesta’s default list has J = 12 factors (Appendix A). The valuer sets: a negotiation coefficient, an annual time trend g, the values of the annexes (parking space and storage room), the weight δj of each factor, the reliability f of each source, the estimator, the confidence level and the rounding step.

3.2 Price cleansing

Each price is turned into the probable price of the dwelling alone at the valuation date:

Pi′=Pi·Kneg,i·Kt,i−Ai(1)
  • Negotiation Kneg,i. The comparable’s own coefficient when the valuer sets one; otherwise 1 for closing prices (land registry, notary, closed sales) and, for asking prices, the valuation’s default, which is 1 when none is set. Valesta does not estimate this coefficient: it is an input of the valuer, whose reasoning the report prints.
  • Time Kt,i. A compound trend over the months mi ≥ 0 between the comparable’s date and the valuation date, with months of 30.4375 days and g limited to [−0.5, 0.5]:
Kt,i=(1+g)mi/12(2)
  • Annexes Ai. The values of the parking space and storage room included in the comparable’s price, so that dwellings are compared with dwellings; the subject’s own annexes are added back in section 3.8.

Updating comparison prices to the valuation date with an index is also the technique of the German regulation (§ 9(1) and § 18) [DE-IMMOWERTV].

3.3 Unit prices and area basis

pi=Pi′Si(3)

The area basis is the built area, the built area with common parts, or the usable area. When a comparable lacks the area of the chosen basis, it is derived from the other with a built-to-usable ratio (1.18 by default). The subject’s area S0 is on the same basis; with the built area with common parts, the comparables’ areas are taken as recorded, and the valuer must make sure that they are on the same basis.

3.4 Homogenisation coefficients

The valuer rates each factor on the scale nij ∈ {−2, −1, 0, +1, +2}: positive when the subject is better than the comparable, so that the comparable’s price is adjusted upwards. Valesta can suggest ratings from the data (area, floor, lift, age, condition, layout, orientation, outdoor space, services, communal areas, energy class), but never for location, and the valuer’s justified ratings prevail. With the weight δj of factor j:

kij=1+nij·δj,Ki=∏jkij,ph,i=pi·Ki(4)

δj is kept in [0, 0.25], so each kij lies in [0.5, 1.5]. With the default weights (Appendix A), Ki ranges from 0.4195 (every rating −2) to 2.2172 (every rating +2). The product makes the order of the factors irrelevant and keeps Ki positive; its cost is that corrections compound (two +5% corrections give +10.25%).

Relation with hedonic prices. Taking logarithms,

lnKi=∑jln(1+nijδj)≈∑jnijδj(5)

which is the hedonic ratio of section 2.1 with Δxij = nij measured on an ordinal scale and βj = δj per step. The analogy makes clear what δj is: an assumed implicit price of one step of the scale, in relative terms. In hedonic practice it would be estimated from transactions [ROSEN-1974] (content to be verified); the German conversion coefficients are derived by official bodies from purchase prices (§ 19) [DE-IMMOWERTV]. In Valesta the defaults are expert values written in the code; the report states for each δj whether it is Valesta’s default or set by the valuer, and the valuer’s source.

3.5 Weights

Comparables that need smaller adjustments carry more information about the subject. Valesta measures the adjustment by the gross adjustment Gi and weights each comparable by the reliability fi of its source and its similarity si:

Gi=∑j|kij−1|=∑j|nij|δj,si=max(0.2,1−Gi),wi=fi·si(6)

The default reliabilities are 1.00 for land-registry, notarial and closed sales, 0.95 for agency mandates, 0.85 for portal listings and 0.80 for other sources. The similarity falls linearly with Gi and reaches its floor of 0.2 at Gi = 0.8; a comparable whose gross adjustment exceeds 35% is flagged. Weights and thresholds are Valesta’s conventions. The Spanish order requires the weighting of comparables to be justified and each weight to be stated (art. 22.1.d) [ES-ECO805]; the report prints every weight.

3.6 Estimators

The unit value p* is one of four estimators of the homogenised prices, the weighted mean being the default:

p∗=∑iwiph,i∑iwi(7)

The alternatives are the arithmetic mean, the median and the trimmed mean, which removes ⌊n·t/2⌋ values from each tail as the TRIMMEAN spreadsheet function does [MS-TRIMMEAN]. The technical report shows the value under every estimator, and the result text states the largest difference between the arithmetic mean, the median and the weighted mean.

3.7 Dispersion and the confidence interval of the estimate

The dispersion of the homogenised prices is measured by the sample standard deviation s and the coefficient of variation CV = s / x̄. The reliability class is high when CV ≤ 10% and n ≥ 6, medium when CV ≤ 15%, and low otherwise; these thresholds are conventions, the sample of six being the minimum of the Spanish order (art. 21.1.c) [ES-ECO805].

For the weighted estimator, unequal weights reduce the information of the sample. Valesta uses the effective sample size of Kish [KISH-1965] (content to be verified) and the weighted variance with the correction for reliability weights of the GNU Scientific Library [GSL-WSTATS]:

neff=(∑iwi)2∑iwi2(8)
sw2=∑iwi(ph,i−p∗)2∑iwi−∑iwi2/∑iwi(9)

The confidence interval of the estimate at level 1 − α uses Student’s t distribution [STUDENT-1908] with ν = max(1, ⌊neff − 1⌋) degrees of freedom:

p∗±h,h=t1−α/2,ν·swneff(10)

For the other estimators, h = t1−α/2, n−1 · s / √n, centred on the chosen estimator; for the median and the trimmed mean this is an approximation, which the report states. The critical values are tabulated for 1 to 30 degrees of freedom; beyond 30, the normal quantiles are used.

What the interval means. Under the assumptions of independent, approximately normal homogenised prices with weights treated as fixed, the procedure covers the mean homogenised unit price of the market segment with probability 1 − α. It measures the precision of the estimate. It is not the range in which the subject’s price would fall: that would be a prediction interval, with half-width of about

hpred≈t1−α/2,ν·sw·1+1neff(11)

which is wider by the factor √(neff + 1) (section 6). Nor does the interval contain the market value «with probability 1 − α»: in the frequentist sense the probability belongs to the procedure, not to one interval. The report therefore calls it the confidence interval of the estimate. The interval also ignores the uncertainty of the inputs set by the valuer, which section 4 addresses.

3.8 From unit value to market value

V=round(p∗·S0+A0−D;r)(12)

where A0 is the value of the subject’s annexes, D the deductions listed by the valuer (for example the cost of regularising an installation) and r the rounding step of the valuation. The bounds of the interval of equation (10) are converted in the same way, and are shown only when the sales comparison enters the value. The listing price is V × (1 + m), rounded to five rounding steps, where m is the margin chosen by the valuer: it is a marketing recommendation, not a value. The basis of value is market value as defined by the International Valuation Standards (IVS 102, Bases of Value) [IVSC-2025].

4. Analysis of the uncertainty due to judgement

4.1 Leave-one-out

For each comparable i in use, the valuation is recomputed without it, and the relative change Δi = (V−i − V) / V is reported. A large |Δi| shows that the value leans on one comparable. Because rounded values are compared, the changes move in steps of one rounding step.

4.2 Monte Carlo simulation of the judgement inputs

The Monte Carlo method studies the output of a model by random sampling of its inputs [METROPOLIS-ULAM-1949] (content to be verified); the metrological guide JCGM 101:2008 describes the propagation of input distributions through a model in this way [JCGM-101-2008]. Valesta applies the technique to the inputs that the valuer sets. In each iteration r = 1, …, N, independently and uniformly:

  • the negotiation coefficient moves within ±0.02 of its value;
  • the time trend is multiplied by a factor in [0.5, 1.5];
  • each annex value is multiplied by a factor in [0.85, 1.15];
  • each δj is multiplied by a factor in [0.8, 1.2];
  • each rating moves by one level, up or down with equal probability, with probability 0.1, within [−2, 2].

The whole valuation is recomputed, giving V(r) before rounding. Valesta reports the mean, the standard deviation and the percentiles P05, P10, P50, P90 and P95 of the simulated values, with linear interpolation between order statistics (definition 7 of [HYNDMAN-FAN-1996], content to be verified), and the spread

hMC=P90−P102V(13)

The report uses N = 600 and a fixed seed of a deterministic pseudo-random generator, so that the same valuation always gives the same figures. The estimate is classed as robust when max |Δi| ≤ 3% and hMC ≤ 5%, moderately sensitive when max |Δi| ≤ 6% and hMC ≤ 10%, and sensitive to the assumptions otherwise.

The ranges and thresholds are conventions of Valesta, not distributions estimated from data. The simulation is therefore a sensitivity analysis in the form of JCGM 101 rather than a measurement of uncertainty in its sense (content to be verified), and the report states that it is not a declaration of material valuation uncertainty.

4.3 Three measures, three questions

MeasureQuestion
Confidence interval of the estimateHow precise is the central estimate, given the dispersion of the homogenised sample?
Leave-one-outDoes the value depend on one comparable?
Monte CarloHow much does the value move when the judgement inputs vary within the stated ranges?

None of them covers errors in the data, the choice of the comparables or the choice of the method.

5. Other approaches and reconciliation

For properties whose value rests on income or cost, Valesta computes three further approaches with the valuer’s inputs:

  • income capitalisation: VI = 12 R (1 − v)(1 − c) / y, with monthly rent R, vacancy v, operating costs c and capitalisation rate y;
  • depreciated replacement cost: VC = L + S · u · (1 + φ)(1 + π)(1 − d), with land value L, unit cost u, fees φ, developer’s profit π and depreciation d;
  • residual: VR = GDV − B − φB − ρ(B + φB) − πGDV − σGDV, with gross development value GDV, building cost B, fees φ, finance ρ, profit π and sale costs σ.

The values are reconciled by the valuer’s weights, normalised over the approaches in use, and the valuer’s reasoning is printed. The international standards and the US literature are understood to expect an explanation of the weight given to each indication [IVSC-2025] [AI-ARE-16] (content to be verified). The discounted cash flow and the profits method, usual for some property types, are not computed; the report names them when they apply.

7. Discussion

7.1 Properties

The method is transparent: each step is a closed formula whose inputs are printed, every coefficient and weight can be traced, and the adjustment table of the report has the comparables in columns and the factors in rows, as in an adjustment grid. It is reproducible: the engine is a pure function of the valuation file, and the simulation uses a fixed seed. It is robust to the order of the factors and keeps prices positive. Its weighting rewards similar comparables without discarding dissimilar ones, and its uncertainty analysis separates the dispersion of the sample from the sensitivity to judgement.

7.2 Limits

  • The default δ are not calibrated. They are a professional starting point, which the valuer should review for each market; the report shows which values are defaults.
  • The ratings are ordinal and partly judgemental. The scale treats the distance between levels as constant, and location is entirely the valuer’s judgement.
  • The coefficients are independent. Interactions between characteristics are captured only through the product.
  • The negotiation coefficient and the time trend are inputs, not estimates.
  • The confidence interval rests on simplifying assumptions: independence, approximate normality, weights treated as fixed, a rule of thumb for the degrees of freedom, and the mean’s half-width for the median and the trimmed mean.
  • The method does not adjust for property rights, financing or conditions of sale, beyond the negotiation coefficient and the choice of comparables.
  • Thresholds are conventions. Only the minimum of six comparables has an external source, the Spanish order.

The International Valuation Standards include a standard on valuation models (IVS 105) [IVSC-2025]; whether Valesta’s documentation meets it must be checked against the text. Conformity of a report with the standards is a statement of its signatory, not a property of the software.

7.3 Calibration and validation

The analogy of equation (5) suggests how δ could be calibrated for a market: by a log-linear hedonic regression of transaction prices on the characteristics, with the ordinal ratings or their underlying measures as regressors [ROSEN-1974] (content to be verified), or by conversion coefficients published by official bodies where they exist [DE-IMMOWERTV]. Expert defaults could then serve as prior values, revised as local evidence accumulates. Independently of calibration, the method should be validated by comparing its values with later transaction prices of the properties valued. Both are outside the scope of this version and are left to the roadmap.

8. Conclusions

Sales comparison with homogenisation coefficients is a compact and transparent form of the market approach: it expresses each difference between the subject and a comparable as a coefficient on an ordinal scale, weights comparables by source and similarity, and reports both the precision of the estimate and its sensitivity to the valuer’s inputs. Its strength is that every number can be explained and reproduced; its main weakness is that the coefficients are assumptions until they are calibrated on market data. Stating both, as this paper does, is a condition for using the method responsibly.
